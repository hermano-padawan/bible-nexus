Start with a clear modern translation and read one Gospel—Mark or John is a good choice—to meet Jesus in the story. Read a short section regularly, ask what it says in context, and write one question or truth. You do not need to begin at Genesis and understand everything on the first attempt.

The Bible is a library, so learning its shape makes the first steps less confusing.

## What is the Bible?

The Bible contains sixty-six books in Protestant editions, with additional Old Testament books in Catholic and Orthodox traditions. These writings include history, law, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, Gospel accounts, letters, and apocalyptic visions.

The Old Testament tells of creation, human rebellion, God’s covenant with Israel, the law, the land, kings, exile, return, worship, and prophetic hope. The New Testament centers on Jesus Christ, the early church, the gospel’s spread, Christian teaching, and the hope of new creation.

Christians believe Scripture is inspired by God, yet it was written through real human authors in particular languages and settings. Reading faithfully involves attention to both divine message and human context.

You are not opening a collection of independent sayings. You are entering a connected story that reaches its center in Christ.

## Choose a translation you can understand

The Old Testament was written mainly in Hebrew, with some Aramaic. The New Testament was written in Greek. Most readers therefore use a translation.

Choose a recognized translation produced with accountable scholarship. English options differ in style. Some stay closer to the structure of the original languages; others prioritize natural contemporary wording.

The World English Bible, New Revised Standard Version, Christian Standard Bible, New International Version, English Standard Version, and New Living Translation represent a range of approaches. Copyright and church-tradition preferences may affect your choice.

The best starting translation is one you can read accurately and consistently. Comparing a second translation can clarify difficult wording. Avoid assuming that older English is automatically more faithful merely because it sounds formal.

## Why begin with a Gospel?

Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John tell the story of Jesus’ ministry, death, and resurrection. Christianity is centered on Him, so a Gospel gives a new reader a clear entry into the Bible’s heart.

Mark is short and moves quickly through Jesus’ actions, teaching, conflict, cross, and resurrection. John is more reflective and explicitly states that it was written so readers may believe Jesus is the Christ and have life in His name.

Luke offers an orderly account with special attention to prayer, the marginalized, and the work of the Holy Spirit; it continues in Acts. Matthew highlights Jesus’ connection with Israel’s Scriptures and organizes substantial teaching.

There is no wrong Gospel with which to begin. Choose one and read it from start to finish rather than jumping randomly each day.

## A simple first reading plan

Read one short section or chapter each day. A sample path is:

1. **Mark** — meet Jesus through a complete Gospel.
2. **Acts** — see how the early church began and the gospel spread.
3. **Genesis 1–25** — learn the beginnings and Abraham’s story.
4. **Exodus 1–20** — follow Israel’s rescue and covenant.
5. **Psalms 1–30** — learn the language of worship, lament, and trust.
6. **Ephesians or Philippians** — read a New Testament letter as a whole.

This is one useful route, not a command. A church reading plan or guidance from a mature Christian may offer another.

Do not postpone the Old Testament forever. The Gospels make deeper sense when you know the promises, images, and history to which they refer.

## Ask three basic questions

After reading, ask:

1. **What happens or what is being said?** Observe before interpreting.
2. **What does this show about God, people, or the gospel?** Stay connected to the actual passage.
3. **What response fits this message?** The response may be trust, repentance, prayer, comfort, or action.

Add a fourth question when something is unclear: **What do I need to learn?** Write it down rather than letting one difficult detail stop the whole reading.

You do not need a profound insight every day. Familiarity grows through repeated contact.

## Read in context

Read the paragraph before and after the verse. Pay attention to who is speaking, to whom, and why. Identify whether you are reading poetry, narrative, a letter, or another genre.

For example, Philippians 4:13—“I can do all things through Christ”—is often treated as a promise of unlimited achievement. The surrounding verses show Paul discussing contentment in hunger and abundance. Christ strengthens him to endure changing conditions faithfully.

Context does not weaken the verse. It reveals its durable meaning.

A study Bible can provide introductions, maps, cross-references, and notes. Read the biblical text first, then use the note as help rather than treating it as inspired.

## What should you do when the Bible is difficult?

Expect difficulty. Some passages describe ancient customs, warfare, law, sexual violence, judgment, or debates far removed from modern life. Confusion does not mean you are incapable or faithless.

Read surrounding chapters. Compare translations. Write the question. Use a reliable commentary or ask a pastor, teacher, or mature Christian who is willing to distinguish what is clear from what is debated.

Do not rush to defend something you do not yet understand. Honest study can say, “I need more context.”

Also distinguish description from approval. The Bible often records harmful actions without endorsing them. A character’s behavior is not automatically an example to copy.

## How much should you read each day?

Read an amount small enough to understand and sustainable enough to repeat. Ten attentive minutes can be more useful than several hurried chapters.

A habit may attach to an existing rhythm: after breakfast, during a commute by audio, at lunch, or before bed. Keep the Bible accessible and reduce distractions.

If you miss a day, continue. The goal is relationship and understanding, not maintaining a flawless streak.

Reading aloud or listening to an audio Bible can help with concentration. Many biblical books were originally heard in communities.

## Should you pray before reading?

Prayer can express humility: “God, help me understand what is true, correct me, and lead me toward Christ.” Psalm 119 repeatedly asks for understanding.

Prayer does not replace careful reading. The Holy Spirit does not need us to ignore grammar, context, or history. Dependence and attention belong together.

After reading, turn the passage into prayer. If it reveals God’s mercy, give thanks. If it exposes sin, confess. If it contains lament, borrow its words for pain.

## Read with other Christians

Scripture belongs to the church, not only private study. Join a responsible church, small group, or class where questions are welcomed and leaders handle the text carefully.

Community helps because other readers notice what you miss. Christians across centuries and cultures also provide wisdom through creeds, sermons, and commentaries.

No human teacher is beyond correction. Compare teaching with Scripture and pay attention when interpretation is used to control, exploit, or silence legitimate questions.

## What if you do not feel anything?

Not every reading produces a strong emotion. Scripture can shape understanding slowly, like regular meals sustain a body without each meal becoming unforgettable.

Do not manufacture a message. Keep reading. Notice one true sentence. Faithfulness is not measured by emotional intensity.

Some days the text will comfort; other days it will confuse or confront. Over time, whole books and themes begin connecting.

## A realistic first step

Choose a Gospel today. Read its introduction in a study Bible, then read the first chapter. Write one sentence about Jesus and one question.

Tomorrow, continue where you stopped. At the end of the book, summarize who Jesus is, what He did, and what response the Gospel invites.

You do not need to master the Bible before it can speak truthfully to you. Begin, remain curious, and let understanding grow.

## Summary

Start with a readable translation and a Gospel. Read short connected sections, observe before applying, respect context, record questions, and seek trustworthy community.

The Bible is learned over a lifetime. A small consistent beginning is not inadequate; it is how a long relationship with Scripture often starts.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I read the Bible from Genesis to Revelation?

You can, but beginners often benefit from starting with a Gospel and then learning the wider story. The Bible’s order is not a required reading plan.

### Which Gospel is easiest for a beginner?

Mark is concise and direct. John is also a strong beginning because it focuses clearly on Jesus’ identity and purpose.

### Do I need a study Bible?

No, but a good one can explain history, geography, and difficult terms. Remember that study notes are human commentary, not Scripture itself.

### What if I disagree with something I read?

Name the difficulty, study the context, and seek thoughtful help. Honest questions are better than pretending. Give the passage time before reaching a final conclusion.
