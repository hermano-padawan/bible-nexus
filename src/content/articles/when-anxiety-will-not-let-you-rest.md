> “Casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you.” — 1 Peter 5:7, World English Bible

Your body is tired, but your mind remains awake. You replay what happened, plan what you will say, and imagine what might go wrong. Worry seems useful because it feels like preparation. Yet after another hour, the problem is no clearer and you are only more tired.

Tonight you do not have to solve your whole life.

## Peter’s invitation begins with God’s care

Peter does not merely say, “Stop worrying.” He says to cast worries on God **because He cares for you**. The invitation rests on God’s character, not on your ability to calm yourself.

To cast a burden means to place its weight somewhere else. You may still remember the problem. You may still need to act tomorrow. But for this night, you can stop treating wakeful thought as the thing that keeps everyone safe.

Name the worry plainly: “Father, I am afraid about the test result.” “I do not know how we will pay this bill.” “I cannot repair that conversation tonight.” Specific words help separate the real concern from the cloud of fear around it.

Then add, “You care for me here.” You may not feel immediate relief. The truth remains available even while your body slowly learns to rest.

## Rest is an act of creaturely trust

Sleep reminds us that we are not God. For several hours, we stop working, watching, and managing. The world continues without our attention.

Psalm 4 says, “In peace I will both lay myself down and sleep, for you alone, Yahweh, make me live in safety.” David’s life included real enemies and danger. His peace was not based on a perfectly controlled environment. It rested in God’s watchful care.

You cannot guarantee that nothing difficult will happen. Biblical peace is not a contract for a trouble-free night. It is permission to place your limited life in the hands of the One who does not sleep.

Rest is not laziness when the body needs it. It is receiving the limit God built into human life.

## Give tomorrow a written place

Sometimes the mind repeats a thought because it is afraid you will forget. Keep paper nearby. Write the task or question in one sentence. If there is an action for tomorrow, write that too: call the doctor, review the account, speak with a supervisor, ask a friend for help.

Now the thought has a place to wait. Tell yourself, “I have not ignored this. I have given it a time.”

Not every worry contains a useful action. If there is nothing you can do now, write, “No action tonight.” That sentence can expose the difference between responsibility and rumination.

Bring the page to God. Ask for wisdom when morning comes. Then set it down.

## Return gently when thoughts wander

Do not turn sleep into another performance. The command “I must fall asleep now” creates new pressure. Instead, return gently to a short prayer.

You might breathe in with the words, “You are here,” and breathe out with, “I am in Your care.” The breath is not magic. It simply gives your attention a quiet place to return.

Philippians 4 encourages believers to bring requests to God and direct their minds toward what is true, honorable, just, pure, lovely, and worthy of praise. Paul wrote from imprisonment. He was not recommending cheerful denial. He was teaching that fear does not deserve unlimited control of our attention.

Choose one true thing: God loves me in Christ. This room is safe right now. I have taken the action available today. I can ask for help tomorrow.

## Care for the body God gave you

Spiritual trust and practical care belong together. Dim the screen. Reduce late caffeine if it affects you. Make the room comfortable. Follow medical advice. A slow stretch, warm drink, or quiet routine may help your body recognize that the day is ending.

If anxiety or sleeplessness continues, speak with a doctor or qualified mental-health professional. Persistent anxiety is not proof of weak faith. Bodies and minds can need skilled care, just as they do with other health concerns.

Tell a trusted person as well. A burden spoken in daylight often feels less powerful at midnight.

Seeking help is not refusing God’s care. It may be one of the ways you receive it.

## God is present even when sleep does not come

Perhaps you follow every suggestion and remain awake. The night is not a spiritual exam you have failed. God is with you in wakefulness too.

Read a familiar psalm without demanding that it produce sleep. Rest your body even if your mind is slow to settle. If appropriate, get up briefly and sit somewhere quiet until drowsiness returns.

The goal is not to force a dramatic experience of peace. It is to stop fighting alone. Christ welcomes the tired person, not only the calm one.

Morning may bring the same unresolved issue. It will also bring new light, possible help, and the opportunity for a real next step. You do not have to take that step in the dark.

## For remembering today

**I do not have to solve tomorrow before I am allowed to rest.**

## Prayer

Father, You know every thought that keeps returning. I place each person, decision, and unfinished task in Your care. Show me what to do when morning comes, but release me from trying to do it now. Quiet my body and guard my mind with the truth of Your love. Help me receive practical care when I need it. If sleep is slow, remain close and keep me from fear. I lie down as Your beloved child. Amen.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does anxiety mean I do not trust God?

No. Anxiety can have many causes, and faithful Christians experience it. Trust may look like praying, taking a wise action, accepting support, and returning to truth each time fear rises.

### Is seeking professional help compatible with faith?

Yes. Doctors and qualified therapists can provide appropriate assessment and care. Prayer, Christian community, healthy habits, and professional treatment can work together.

### What if prayer does not calm me immediately?

Prayer is still worthwhile because it brings your real need into relationship with God. Do not judge yourself by an instant feeling. Continue with gentle practices and seek help when symptoms persist.
